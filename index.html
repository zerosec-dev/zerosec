Zero Trust: The Overhead You Cut Is the Understanding You Lose

14 Sep 2026

At every program, and at every layer of management, the same instinct shows up: reduce risk by moving the work somewhere else. Offload it to a vendor, a managed service, a support contract offshore. The billets go away, the training line goes away, and risk looks smaller on the slide.

It does not work for zero trust. Zero trust is not a box you install. It is an authorization decision your systems make on every request, from the user's claims and the device's current state. Outsource that and the risk stays where it was. Only the understanding moved.

The cost shows up in development, not in the invoice. Every change becomes a vendor request. A new service needs an authorization rule, so you file a ticket and wait. The engineer who writes the rule needs a clearance, and so does the engineer who reviews it, so the pool of people who can touch your policy is small on both sides and billed by the hour.

Then the requirement goes through translation. Your team describes the data flow, the vendor implements what they heard, and the mismatch surfaces two sprints later as a denial nobody expected. Troubleshooting runs the same way: your engineer reproduces it, the vendor engineer cannot see your enclave, and the loop closes over screenshots and a shared call. Mean time to repair is now someone else's queue depth.

Zoom out and the pattern is circular. The prime bills the program, subcontracts the zero trust scope, and the sub buys a licence from a vendor who bills for the integration hours to make it fit. Each hop adds margin and a coordination meeting, and none of them adds an engineer on your side who understands the policy. It has the same shape as the circular investment deals in AI right now, except the money is appropriated, and the deliverable is a capability the program still cannot operate alone.

Who needs this. Defense programs, DIB suppliers under DFARS 252.204-7012, and any enclave that runs air-gapped, disconnected, or at CUI and above. If your sites hold a reliable circuit and your data is unregulated, a SASE subscription is probably the cheaper answer and you can stop reading.

TL;DR.

  • Outsourcing zero trust cuts headcount, not overhead. The expertise leaves with the billets.
  • Real overhead is manual work: three directories to maintain, certificates rotated by hand, procedure held in one person's memory. Automate that.
  • An operator who can read the policy and restore the control plane is not overhead. That operator is the control.
  • Three documents prove you still own the decision: the written trust algorithm, a replayable decision record, and a control mapping. Ask your vendor for all three.
  • The offline test is one sequence: unplug the uplink, authenticate, call a service. Most deployments fail it.
  • We assess the decision path, redesign what is missing, build it with your engineers on open source, and hand over the runbooks and the skill.

1. Where the understanding went

Follow one request from a user's card to your data, and mark who owns each hop. The hops marked "vendor" are the ones your team stopped learning the day the product was bought.

  request path                          typical managed ZT    self-hosted
  ------------------------------------  --------------------  --------------
  user + CAC/PIV card                   you                   you
    |
    v
  device posture agent                  vendor, closed        osquery+FleetDM
    |
    v
  overlay / tunnel                      vendor PoP or box     WireGuard/Nebula
    |
    v
  PEP  - permits or denies the call     vendor                Envoy, your conf
    |
    +--> PDP - policy engine + trust
    |         algorithm                 vendor, black box     OPA + Rego in git
    |          ^
    |          |
    |         signals: identity,
    |         posture, PKI, time        vendor                Keycloak, SPIRE,
    |                                                         Vault, FreeIPA
    v
  resource - your data, your API        you                   you
    |
    v
  decision record / evidence            vendor console        ClickHouse, yours

Fig 1. Ownership of the decision path. Every hop marked "vendor" is a hop you cannot inspect, replay, or run disconnected.

Draw the boundary explicitly. The enforcement decision is either inside the enclave you are accredited to defend, or it is not. Everything else in the argument follows from that line.

2. Methodology

A tool list is not a method. For each of the seven DoD pillars we check three properties against the path in Fig 1, then drill down wherever one fails.

  • Ownership. Can your staff change the rule without a vendor ticket?
  • Evidence. Does a decision produce a record an assessor can read, months later, without vendor assistance?
  • Continuity. Does the decision still happen with the WAN down, the console unreachable, and the licence server unreachable?

Drill-down order is broad to narrow: estate boundary, then pillar, then one decision path, then one denied request. A finding is only real when it lands on a specific request.

3. Triage, in three layers

Layers are ordered by cost and invasiveness. Most findings surface in Layer 1.

Layer 1 — inventory and configuration

Cheap, read-only, no production impact. Token lifetimes, certificate TTLs, posture intervals, overlay ACLs, log retention. We are looking for standing access: a credential with no expiry, a flat segment, a group that grants a resource directly. Where a managed product sits in the path, this is also where we request the three documents: trust algorithm, one decision record, control mapping. A missing document is a Layer 1 finding on its own.

Layer 2 — the decision path

Trace one real request end to end and read the logs it produced. This is where the gap between the architecture diagram and the running system appears, usually as an enforcement point that was never placed in front of a legacy resource.

Layer 3 — adversarial and offline tests

Cut the uplink. Expire a posture feed. Revoke a card mid-session. Rotate the CA. These are scheduled tests in a maintenance window, not production experiments; each one answers a question the console cannot.

4. Reading the output

Findings cite commands and output, not impressions. Three examples from a self-hosted stack:

# Replay a denied request against the exact bundle that produced it.
$ opa eval --explain fails -b bundles/authz-1.14.2.tar.gz \
      -i decisions/2026-09-03T0314Z.json 'data.envoy.authz.allow'
false
  posture.ts_ns stale by 1140s   # <- the rule that denied it

# Workload credentials actually issued, and their TTLs.
$ spire-server entry show | grep -E 'spiffe_id|ttl'

# Posture freshness across the fleet.
:) SELECT quantile(0.99)(now() - ts) AS p99_stale_s FROM posture;
p99_stale_s: 1140

Read the last one carefully. The posture interval is 300 s, so a p99 staleness of 1140 s means policy is deciding on facts nearly four intervals old. Normal is under 600 s. Above that, treat every allow as suspect: a non-reporting agent looks identical to a healthy one unless staleness is alarmed. That is the next section.

5. Gotchas

  • A green console is not enforcement. Dashboards report agent check-in, not whether a PEP sits in front of every resource. Enumerate resources, then find each one's enforcement point. The ones with none are the finding.
  • Posture agents fail open. If a stale fact is not a failed fact, an attacker's move is to stop the agent. Alarm on staleness, not only on non-compliance.
  • Average latency hides the denial tail. Users experience the outliers. Read p99 on the authorization path, and never sample the decision log.
  • Vendor telemetry is an egress channel you approved. Hostnames, usernames, and destination graphs are often the sensitive part on a CUI program. "Metadata only" is not a control you can put in an SSP.
  • Long-lived credentials undo the design. A one-year certificate on disk is standing access with extra steps. If a leaked overlay key reaches a resource, the architecture has collapsed back to a VPN.

6. Resolution and verification

The fix is usually structural: move the decision inside the boundary, place enforcement in front of every resource, and make policy a text file under version control. Components we deploy are open source and self-hosted — Keycloak and FreeIPA for user identity, osquery and FleetDM for posture, SPIRE for workload identity, WireGuard or Nebula for the overlay, Envoy as the PEP, OPA and Rego as the policy engine, Vault for PKI, Vector and ClickHouse for the record.

Verification is a checklist, and it belongs in your runbook whether or not we run it:

[ ] Unplug the uplink. Authenticate a user. Call a service.
[ ] Pick one denial from last week. Replay it offline. Name the rule.
[ ] Expire a posture feed. Confirm policy denies instead of failing open.
[ ] Rotate the workload CA. Confirm no service outage.
[ ] Export 90 days of decision records without vendor assistance.
[ ] Hand the trust algorithm to an engineer who did not write it.
    Ask them to explain one rule.

Six passes means you own the decision path. Any failure names the next piece of work.
